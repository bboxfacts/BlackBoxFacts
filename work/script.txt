The fizz in your soda isn't the bubbles. It's acid.

Everyone thinks that sharp bite is tiny bubbles popping on your tongue.

Nope. Scientists sat people in a pressure chamber where bubbles physically can't form.

Same fizz. Basically the same bite. Zero bubbles.

So what are you actually feeling? Here's the insane part.

Your tongue is coated with an enzyme called carbonic anhydrase.

It grabs the carbon dioxide dissolved in your drink and snaps it into carbonic acid, almost instantly.

That acid lights up your sour taste cells, and it pokes your pain nerves too.

So the fizz is literally your mouth making acid, then hurting itself with it.

But it gets weirder.

Mountain climbers take a pill for altitude sickness that blocks that exact enzyme.

And suddenly soda, beer, even champagne taste flat. Doctors actually named it the champagne blues.

The bubbles are still right there, fizzing in the glass. They just stopped mattering.

Bubbles do help a little. They drag the acid around your tongue and make the bite stronger.

But they're the sidekick, not the star.

So next time you crack open a can, just remember.
