Your cinnamon probably isn't real cinnamon, and it contains a banned chemical.

Most of what's sold in stores is cassia, a cousin tree from China and Indonesia.

True cinnamon comes from Sri Lanka, and it's called Ceylon.

Here's how to tell. Grab a stick.

Cassia is one thick, rock-hard curl of bark.

Ceylon is dozens of paper-thin layers, rolled up like a cigar.

But here's the insane part.

Cassia is packed with coumarin, the chemical that makes fresh-cut hay smell sweet.

In 1954, the FDA banned adding coumarin to food.

But it's still in cassia, because the tree makes it naturally.

And it gets worse.

In the 1920s, cows started bleeding to death after eating moldy sweet clover hay.

The mold had fused two coumarin molecules into a blood thinner.

Scientists built on that molecule to make rat poison, which became the blood thinner warfarin.

Coumarin itself doesn't thin your blood, but big doses can stress your liver.

Germany's risk agency says just two grams of cassia a day hits the safety limit for a small adult.

A sprinkle on your toast is fine.
